# Minnesota Adverse Possession: One-Page Summary (for Kevin)

Prepared 2026-11-21 from sources read in this session. Research summary only, not legal advice. For a real property dispute, consult a licensed Minnesota attorney.

## The rule in brief
To win title by adverse possession in Minnesota, the claimant must show, by **clear and convincing evidence**, possession that is **actual, open, hostile, continuous, and exclusive** for **15 years**.
- Source for elements and standard: Ehle v. Prosser (quoted below). Repeated in Rogers v. Moore.
- Source for the 15 years: Minn. Stat. 541.02.

The statute does NOT list the five elements. Those come from case law.

Correction: 559.23 is the lawsuit procedure for fixing a boundary. It does NOT use the words "practical location." That doctrine is common law (508.02 calls it the "common law doctrine of practical location of boundaries"). Ehle mentioned 559.23 but did not decide a practical-boundary claim.

## Cases (both read in full on CourtListener)

**Ehle v. Prosser, 293 Minn. 183, 197 N.W.2d 458 (1972)**
- Minnesota Supreme Court (state's highest court). Published: likely, has an official Minnesota Reports cite. [Likely]
- Held: payment of taxes for 5 years is not an absolute prerequisite; landowners proved adverse possession of a 37-foot strip. Trial court reversed.
- Quoted: "the disseizor must show, by clear and convincing evidence, an actual, open, hostile, continuous, and exclusive possession for the requisite period of time which, under our statute, is 15 years."
- Quoted: "Subjective intent to take land adversely is not essential in this state ... so long as he does intend to exclude all others."
- Quoted: "It is sufficient that the land is occupied by mistake."
- Hostile means intent "to claim exclusive ownership as against the world" (paraphrased from the opinion), not personal animosity.
- Silence by the owner is "acquiescence," not "permission."

**Rogers v. Moore, 603 N.W.2d 650 (Minn. 1999)**
- Minnesota Supreme Court. Published: likely. [Likely]
- A prescriptive EASEMENT case (a driveway), not pure adverse possession. It states that the elements are the same for both, "subject to such differences as are necessarily inherent."
- Held: district court's finding of continuous use was not clearly erroneous; court of appeals reversed.
- Quoted: "The elements necessary to establish adverse possession must be proved by clear and convincing evidence."
- Footnote (paraphrased): registered (Torrens) land cannot be taken by adverse possession or prescription.

## Practical points (from the cases)
- A mistaken belief about where the boundary is does not defeat the claim.
- Improving and maintaining the land (landscaping, driveway, signs) is evidence of possession.
- Permission from the owner defeats the claim; silent acquiescence does not.
- Torrens (registered) land cannot be taken this way. Check how the land is recorded.

## Limits
